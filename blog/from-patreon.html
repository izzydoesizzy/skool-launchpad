Switching from Patreon to Skool

By Izzy Piyale-Sheard · April 14, 2026

Patreon was a game-changer when it launched. Finally, creators could get paid directly by their fans. Monthly support, tiered rewards, a direct relationship with your audience. Beautiful concept.

But the cracks are showing. Fees keep climbing. Community features are limited. Content gets buried in a reverse-chronological feed that nobody scrolls back through. And there's no way to host courses or structured content.

If you're building more than a tip jar, Skool gives you a different kind of relationship with your audience. Let's talk about what changes.

Why people leave Patreon

$
Platform fees are high and getting higher. Patreon now charges a flat 10% platform fee for new creators (as of August 2025). Add payment processing (roughly 3%) and you're losing 12-15% of every dollar your patrons pay. On iOS, it's worse: Apple takes 30% on in-app purchases, and Patreon bumps iOS prices by about 43% to cover it. That creates pricing confusion for your members.
👥
Limited community features. Patreon's community is basically a content feed. Posts go up, they scroll down, they disappear. No threaded discussions. No gamification. No real way for members to interact with each other. It's a one-to-many broadcast, not a community.
📚
No course hosting. If you want to teach something structured, Patreon doesn't help. You can post individual videos or PDFs, but there's no course builder, no modules, no progress tracking. Content just lives in the feed with everything else.
🔍
Content gets buried. Posted a great resource three months ago? Good luck finding it. Patreon's feed is purely chronological. No categories, no search that works well, no way to organize your best content so members can find it later.

What you'll lose

Podcast RSS feeds. If you use Patreon to deliver a private podcast to paying members, Skool can't replace that. You'd need a separate podcast hosting service (like Supercast or Hello Audio) to maintain private RSS feeds alongside your Skool community.

Merch integration. Patreon connects with merch platforms so patrons can receive physical goods at certain tiers. Skool is digital-only. If merch is part of your patron rewards, you'll need to handle fulfillment separately.

The "Patreon" brand association. Some audiences specifically look for creators on Patreon. The platform has its own marketplace and culture. Moving off it means you're no longer discoverable within that ecosystem. For most creators, this isn't a big deal. But if a meaningful chunk of your patrons found you through Patreon's browse feature, factor that in.

What you'll gain

  • Real community. Not a content feed. An actual community where members interact, help each other, and build relationships. This is the single biggest upgrade.
  • Courses and structured content. Take all those patron-only tutorials, workshops, and resources and organize them into proper courses. Members can track their progress and find things easily.
  • Gamification. Points, levels, leaderboards. Members earn rewards for participating. This drives daily engagement in a way Patreon's feed never could.
  • Lower fees. Skool Pro charges 2.9% on transactions. Patreon takes 10% plus processing. On $5,000/mo in revenue, that's the difference between $145 and $650 in fees. More on that below.
  • Content that doesn't disappear. Everything has a place. Community posts are categorized. Course content is structured. No more "I know I posted that somewhere" moments.

The break-even point

At around $1,000/mo in revenue, the costs are roughly equal. Below that, Patreon is cheaper because you're not paying a flat $99/mo subscription.

Above $1,000/mo, Skool wins. And the gap widens fast. At $10,000/mo, you're saving nearly $11,000/year. That's real money.

One more thing: these Patreon numbers assume web-based sign-ups. If your patrons sign up through the iOS app, Patreon bumps the price to cover Apple's 30% cut. That means either your patrons pay more (and some won't) or you absorb the difference.

How to migrate (step by step)

  1. 1
    Export your patron list. In Patreon's creator dashboard, download your patron list as CSV. Get names, emails, tier levels, and join dates. This is your migration contact list.
  2. 2
    Build your Skool community first. Sign up here. Set up your community, organize your best content into courses in the Classroom, seed the community feed with a few posts. Make it feel alive before you invite anyone.
  3. 3
    Announce the migration on Patreon. Post a detailed update explaining why you're moving. Be transparent about the fees and what's better on the new platform. Give your patrons at least 2-3 weeks notice. People don't like surprises with their subscriptions.
  4. 4
    Offer a founding member rate. Give your existing patrons a discount or a locked-in rate on Skool. This rewards loyalty and reduces friction. "You supported me early. Here's your reward: the same price (or lower) for life."
  5. 5
    Email your patrons directly. Don't rely on the Patreon post alone. Send personal emails (or use your email list) with a direct link to your Skool community. Make it one click to join.
  6. 6
    Run both for 30 days. This is a longer overlap than a typical platform switch. Patreon members are used to monthly billing cycles, so give them a full cycle to make the transition. Post on both platforms during this period. On Patreon, every post should mention the move.
  7. 7
    Wind down Patreon. After 30 days, post a final update with the Skool link. Pause your Patreon page (don't delete it right away, in case stragglers come through). Keep the page up with a pinned post directing people to your new home.

A note on patron psychology

Here's something most migration guides skip. Patreon has a specific emotional dynamic. People "support" creators. It feels like patronage, like they're helping you exist. That's different from "joining a community" or "buying a membership."

When you move to Skool, reframe the value. It's not "support me." It's "join us." Emphasize what they get: community, courses, live access, a peer group. The shift from patron to member is healthy for your business, but acknowledge that some people liked the old relationship. Give them a reason to be excited about the new one.

When to stay on Patreon

Patreon is still the better fit if:

  • • Your audience expects a Patreon-style relationship (patronage, not membership)
  • • You rely on Patreon's marketplace for discovering new patrons
  • • Private podcast RSS feeds are central to your offering
  • • You integrate merch rewards into your tiers
  • • You're earning under $1,000/mo (Patreon's percentage model is cheaper at lower revenue)

Patreon works well as a tip jar or support mechanism. Skool works better when you're building a real membership business with community, courses, and recurring engagement.

The bottom line

Patreon helped a generation of creators get paid for their work. That matters. But the platform hasn't kept up with what creators need in 2026. The fees are high, the community tools are thin, and the content model (everything in a feed) doesn't scale.

Skool gives you a place where your members connect with each other, not just with you. Where your content stays organized and findable. Where you keep more of what you earn.

The switch takes about a month when you include the overlap period. Most creators report that 60-80% of their patrons make the move. And the ones who do tend to be more engaged than they ever were on Patreon, because now they have a community, not just a subscription.
