Skool vs Teachable: When to Use Which

By Izzy Piyale-Sheard · April 14, 2026

Skool and Teachable both let you sell courses. But that's where the similarity ends.

Teachable is a course sales machine. It gives you polished sales pages, checkout flows, upsells, and marketing funnels. It's built to sell a course to a stranger on the internet.

Skool is a community platform that happens to include courses. It's built to keep people together, talking, learning, and paying monthly.

Same category on paper. Totally different tools in practice. Let's walk through when each one makes sense.

The core difference

Teachable treats courses as products. You build a course, create a sales page, drive traffic, collect payment, deliver the content. The student might never interact with you or other students again. It's a transaction.

Skool treats courses as one part of a community experience. Members join your group, participate in discussions, attend live calls, work through your course material, and climb a leaderboard. The course lives inside the community. It's a relationship.

Neither approach is wrong. But they serve very different business models.

Feature comparison

Course builder

Teachable has the more mature course builder. Drip scheduling (release lessons over time), quizzes, completion certificates, student progress tracking, and compliance tools for regulated industries. If you're selling a structured educational product, Teachable gives you the polish and the tools to back it up.

Skool's course builder is functional but simpler. Modules, lessons, videos. No certificates. No quizzes. No drip scheduling. It works well for community-based learning where the course is a resource members access, not a standalone product with formal completion requirements.

Community

This is Skool's home turf. Every Skool group has a community feed where members post, comment, and interact. There's a leaderboard with points and levels. Members earn rewards for engagement. It's active and alive by design.

Teachable added community features, but they're basic. You get discussion channels attached to your courses. Students can post and reply. But it feels like a quiet forum bolted onto a course platform, not a vibrant community space. There's no gamification, no leaderboard, no real social energy built into the product.

If community is central to your offer, Skool wins this one by a wide margin.

Marketing and sales tools

Teachable was built for selling. You get dedicated sales pages for each course, coupon codes, order bumps, upsells, and an affiliate program. The checkout experience is clean. You can run promotions without needing a third-party tool.

Skool has none of this. No sales pages. No funnels. No coupons. No order bumps. If someone visits your Skool group, they see a description and a "Join" button. That's the entire sales experience. You'll need to build landing pages and funnels elsewhere if you want a polished sales flow.

Payments and pricing models

Teachable supports one-time payments, subscriptions, and payment plans. You can sell a $497 course, a $29/month membership, or a 3-pay plan. Lots of flexibility for how you price your products.

Skool focuses on recurring memberships. You set a monthly or annual price for your group. Members pay to access everything: community, courses, events. It's simpler, but it means you can't easily sell a one-time course without bundling it into a membership.

Certificates

Teachable offers completion certificates on every plan. Students finish a course, they get a branded certificate they can download or share. If you're in professional development, corporate training, or continuing education, this matters.

Skool doesn't offer certificates. Period.

Analytics

Teachable provides detailed analytics: revenue reports, student progress, completion rates, traffic sources, and conversion data. You can see exactly where students drop off in your course and which marketing channels bring buyers.

Skool's analytics are more basic. You can see member activity, engagement metrics, and revenue. But you won't get the granular course-level or funnel-level data that Teachable provides.

Pricing breakdown

Skool has two plans:

  • Hobby: $9/month. Unlimited members, courses, and videos. One admin. 10% transaction fee on paid memberships.
  • Pro: $99/month. Same features. Unlimited admins. 2.9% transaction fee.

No annual contracts. No feature gating. Both plans include everything Skool offers.

Teachable has four paid plans (annual pricing):

  • Starter: $29/month. 1 published product, up to 100 students, 7.5% transaction fee. Basic features only.
  • Builder: $69/month. 5 products, 1,000 students, 0% transaction fees. Adds email marketing and affiliates.
  • Growth: $139/month. 25 products, white-label branding, student translations.
  • Advanced: $309/month. 100+ products, unlimited integrations, higher API limits.

A few things jump out. Teachable's Starter plan caps you at 100 students and still takes 7.5% of every sale. If you're just getting started, that fee adds up fast. Skool's Hobby plan at $9/month gives you unlimited members with a 10% fee, but there's no student cap.

At the mid-tier, Teachable's Builder plan ($69/month) drops transaction fees to 0% but limits you to 5 products and 1,000 students. Skool Pro ($99/month) gives you unlimited everything at 2.9%.

The right choice depends on volume. If you're selling high-ticket courses ($500+) to a smaller audience, Teachable's 0% transaction fee saves you more than Skool's 2.9%. If you're running a $29/month membership with hundreds of members, Skool's flat pricing makes more sense.

The hybrid approach

Here's something most comparison posts won't tell you: you don't have to choose one.

A lot of successful creators use both. Teachable for standalone course sales. Skool for ongoing community and membership.

The setup looks like this:

  • Sell your flagship course on Teachable. Use their sales pages, checkout flows, and payment plans to maximize conversions on a one-time purchase.
  • Run your community on Skool. Monthly membership. Live calls. Discussions. Gamification. This is where the recurring revenue lives.
  • Link them together. Invite Teachable course buyers into your Skool community as a bonus or upsell. Now your one-time buyers become monthly subscribers.

This gives you the best of both worlds: Teachable's sales infrastructure for courses, Skool's engagement engine for community. Two revenue streams from one audience.

Who picks Skool

Skool is the better fit if you're building:

  • A coaching or membership business. Your value is ongoing access, not a one-time course. Members stay because of the community, the live calls, and you.
  • A community-first brand. The course is a resource inside the community, not the main product. Members come for the people and the conversations.
  • A group program with accountability. The leaderboard and gamification keep members showing up. Peer pressure works. So does friendly competition.
  • Something on a tight budget. $9/month to get started. No annual commitment. Hard to beat.

Who picks Teachable

Teachable is the better fit if you're building:

  • Standalone courses for sale. You create a course, build a sales page, run ads, sell it. The student buys, completes the content, and moves on. No ongoing community needed.
  • A course business with sales funnels. You need landing pages, order bumps, upsells, coupon codes, and affiliate programs. Teachable has these built in. Skool doesn't.
  • Professional or certified education. You need completion certificates, quizzes, drip content, and student progress tracking. Teachable handles all of this.
  • Multiple distinct courses at different price points. Teachable lets you sell each course separately with its own pricing. On Skool, everything is bundled into one membership.

The bottom line

If your business revolves around community, coaching, or monthly memberships, pick Skool. The engagement tools, simplicity, and pricing are hard to beat. You can be live in an afternoon.

If your business is selling courses as products, pick Teachable. The sales infrastructure, certificates, and analytics are built for that model.

And if you're doing both? Use both. Teachable for sales. Skool for retention. The tools aren't competitors. They're complements.
