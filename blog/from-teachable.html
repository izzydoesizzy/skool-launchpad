Switching from Teachable to Skool

By Izzy Piyale-Sheard · April 14, 2026

Teachable was one of the first course platforms a lot of us ever used. It made selling online courses simple. But if you've been on Teachable for a while, you've probably noticed something: the pricing keeps climbing, the community features are basically nonexistent, and your students finish a course and then... disappear.

That's the gap Skool fills. Let's break down what changes when you switch, what stays the same, and how to move everything without losing students along the way.

Why people leave Teachable

Nobody wakes up one day and decides to migrate platforms for fun. There's usually a breaking point. Here are the most common ones I hear:

$
Pricing tiers got expensive. Teachable's Starter plan is $39/mo (monthly billing), but it charges a 7.5% transaction fee on every sale. To get rid of that fee, you need the Builder plan at $69/mo. Growth is $139/mo. Advanced is $309/mo. These costs add up fast, especially when you realize you're mostly using the course hosting.
👥
No community built in. Teachable is a course delivery tool. There's no community feed, no discussion forum that feels alive, no gamification. Students log in, watch videos, and leave. You have to bolt on a separate community tool (Facebook Group, Discord, Circle) just to keep people engaged.
📈
Low student engagement. Course completion rates on standalone platforms hover around 5-15%. Without a community pushing people forward, most students buy and never finish. That means fewer testimonials, fewer referrals, fewer repeat buyers.
⚡
No recurring revenue model. Teachable is built around one-time course sales. You can set up subscriptions, but the platform doesn't encourage membership thinking. You're always chasing the next launch instead of building monthly recurring revenue.

What you'll lose (and whether it matters)

Let's be honest about the gaps.

Sales funnels and marketing automation. Teachable lets you build multi-step sales pages, order bumps, upsells, and coupon workflows. Skool doesn't do any of that. If your business depends on complex sales funnels, you'll need a separate tool (or this might not be the right move for you).

Advanced quiz and assessment tools. Teachable has native quizzes with grading. Skool doesn't. For most community-based courses, this isn't a deal-breaker. But if you run certification programs where assessments are core, you'll feel the gap.

Standalone course marketplace. Teachable courses can be discovered through search engines and Teachable's own marketplace. Skool does have a discovery feature, but it's community-focused, not course-focused. Your courses live inside your community, not as standalone products.

What you'll gain

This is where the switch pays off.

  • A real community. Feed, discussions, direct messages, group calls. Your students stick around instead of vanishing after the last lesson.
  • Gamification. Points, levels, leaderboards. Members earn rewards for engaging, which drives course completion naturally.
  • Live events. Skool Call lets you run live sessions with up to 10,000 people. No Zoom link needed.
  • Recurring revenue. Skool is membership-first. Monthly subscriptions are the default, not an afterthought.
  • Simpler everything. One dashboard. Community, courses, events, payments. No plugin fatigue.

How to migrate (step by step)

  1. 1
    Export your student list from Teachable. Go to Users in your Teachable admin. Export the full CSV. This is your lifeline for communicating the switch. Save course enrollment data too so you know who bought what.
  2. 2
    Download all your course content. Videos, PDFs, worksheets, any files you uploaded. Teachable doesn't make bulk export easy, so work through it course by course. Don't skip this step.
  3. 3
    Create your Skool community. Sign up here and set up your community. Name it, write your description, upload your branding.
  4. 4
    Rebuild your courses in Skool's Classroom. Create modules and lessons. Upload videos (Skool hosts them natively). Add your PDFs and resources as attachments. The structure will be simpler than Teachable, and that's fine. Simpler often means more students actually finish.
  5. 5
    Email your students about the move. Be direct. Tell them why (better experience, community, live access to you). Give them a specific date. Make it easy: one link, one click to join. Offer a perk for early movers if you can.
  6. 6
    Run both platforms in parallel for 2 weeks. Don't pull the rug. Keep Teachable active while students transition. Post reminders in both places. After 2 weeks, send a final "we're closing Teachable on [date]" email.
  7. 7
    Cancel your Teachable subscription. Once everyone's moved and you've confirmed all content is accessible on Skool, downgrade or cancel Teachable. Keep your exported data backed up.

When to stay on Teachable

Teachable is the better choice if:

  • • You rely on advanced sales funnels, order bumps, and upsells to drive revenue
  • • You sell standalone courses to cold traffic and don't need community features
  • • You need native quizzes and certificates for compliance or certification programs
  • • You want a standalone course marketplace where new students discover you
  • • Your business model is built around one-time course sales, not memberships

Teachable is a course sales machine. Skool is a community with courses inside it. Different tools for different models. Pick the one that matches how you want to run your business.

The bottom line

If you're on Teachable and your students are ghosting after they finish a course, if you're paying for features you don't use, or if you want to build something more than a course catalog, Skool is worth a serious look.

The migration isn't instant. Budget a weekend to rebuild your courses and a couple of weeks to transition your students. But once you're on the other side, you've got a platform where people actually show up, engage, and stick around.

That's the difference between selling a course and building a community. And the second one is a much better business.
