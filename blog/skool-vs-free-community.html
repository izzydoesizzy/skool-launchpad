Why Pay for Skool When Facebook Groups Are Free?

By Izzy Piyale-Sheard · April 14, 2026

This is the question I hear more than any other. And it's a fair one.

Facebook Groups cost nothing. Discord is free. Slack has a free tier. Why would anyone pay $99 a month for Skool when these platforms exist?

I get it. When you're starting out, every dollar matters. But after running communities on Facebook, Discord, and Skool, I can tell you: "free" is the most expensive option most people choose. Let me explain why.

You don't own your audience on free platforms

This is the big one. On Facebook, your group lives on Meta's property. They own the house. You're just renting a room.

That means:

  • Algorithm changes can bury your posts. Facebook has been throttling organic reach in Groups for years. Many admins report that only 10-20% of their members even see new posts.
  • Groups get shut down without warning. Violated a policy you didn't know existed? Got mass-reported by a competitor? Your group can vanish overnight. And there's no real customer support to call.
  • You can't export your member list. You don't have email addresses. You don't have phone numbers. If the group disappears, your community disappears with it.

On Skool, you own the member data. Email addresses. Join dates. Payment history. If you ever decide to leave, you take your people with you.

That's not a small thing. That's everything.

The workaround tax

Free platforms are missing core features that community builders need. So you end up duct-taping together a stack of other tools to fill the gaps.

Running a "free" community on Facebook usually looks like this:

  • Facebook Group for discussion
  • Teachable or Thinkific for courses ($39-99/mo)
  • Stripe or PayPal for collecting payments
  • Calendly for scheduling calls ($12/mo)
  • Zoom for live events ($13-20/mo)
  • Mailchimp or ConvertKit for email ($20-50/mo)
  • Zapier to connect everything ($20-50/mo)

Add that up. You're looking at $100-250 per month in tools, plus the hours you spend getting them to talk to each other. And when something breaks (Zapier connections love to break), you're the IT department.

Skool rolls community, courses, events, payments, and gamification into one platform for $99/mo. One login. One dashboard. One bill.

The "free" platform suddenly costs more than the paid one.

No built-in payments means lost revenue

Facebook Groups have no native payment processing. If you want to charge for access, you need a separate checkout page, a separate billing system, and some way to manually add or remove members when payments succeed or fail.

Every extra step in that process loses people. Someone clicks your link, gets sent to a Stripe checkout page, then has to wait for a manual invite to the group. Friction kills conversions.

Skool handles payments natively. Someone clicks "Join," enters their card, and they're in. Instantly. No manual steps, no waiting, no lost sales because your Zapier automation didn't fire.

It also handles failed payments, cancellations, and free trials automatically. That's revenue you'd lose on a manual system.

The perception problem

Here's something people don't talk about enough: a Facebook Group feels like a Facebook Group. It carries all the baggage of the platform. Memes in the sidebar. Marketplace ads. Your aunt's vacation photos one tab over.

If you're charging $49, $99, or $500 a month for a coaching program, the container matters. A dedicated platform signals: "This is a professional space. Your investment is going somewhere real."

Discord has a similar problem. It was built for gamers. Running a business coaching community on Discord is like hosting a corporate retreat in a laser tag arena. It can work, but the vibe is off.

Skool's interface is clean, focused, and distraction-free. No ads. No algorithmic feeds pulling attention away. Members log in and the only thing they see is your community.

No gamification, no engagement loop

One of Skool's most underrated features is its built-in gamification system. Members earn points for posting, commenting, and completing course modules. Points unlock levels. Levels unlock perks you define (private channels, bonus content, 1-on-1 calls).

This creates a self-reinforcing engagement loop. People participate because they see progress. They see progress because they participate.

Facebook has no equivalent. Discord has roles, but assigning them is manual. Neither platform gives you a leaderboard that motivates members to show up and contribute.

Communities live or die on engagement. Gamification isn't a gimmick. It's infrastructure.

The moderation headache

Moderating a Facebook Group is a part-time job. Spam bots. Promotional posts. Members who can't find the search button. The notification settings alone are enough to drive you crazy.

Discord is even worse. Channel sprawl turns your "simple community" into a maze of rooms that nobody navigates. New members join, feel overwhelmed, and quietly disappear.

Skool keeps moderation simple. Four tabs (Community, Classroom, Calendar, Members). Clean categories. Admin controls that don't require a computer science degree. Less time moderating means more time actually helping your members.

When free platforms actually make sense

I'd be lying if I said Skool was the right choice for everyone. Free platforms genuinely work well in certain situations:

  • Pure networking groups where nobody is monetizing. Alumni groups, local meetups, hobby clubs. If nobody's paying and nobody's selling, Facebook Groups do the job fine.
  • Very early validation. If you're testing whether anyone cares about your topic, a free Facebook Group is a fast, zero-risk way to find out. Prove the concept, then move to Skool.
  • Audiences that live on Facebook. If your people are 55+ and already spend their day on Facebook, meet them where they are. Asking them to create a Skool account might be too much friction.

If your community is purely social with no courses, no paid tiers, and no business model? Free works. But the moment you want to teach, charge, or build something more structured, free platforms start holding you back.

Let's do the math

Skool Pro costs $99/mo. Sounds like a lot until you do basic arithmetic.

Say you charge $49/mo for your community. You need 3 paying members to cover Skool's cost. Three. That's it.

With 10 members at $49/mo, you're bringing in $490/mo. Subtract Skool's $99 and you're netting $391/mo from a community that runs itself with built-in payments, courses, and engagement tools.

With 50 members? That's $2,450/mo. With 100? Almost $5,000/mo.

And if you're not ready to charge yet, Skool's Hobby plan starts at just $9/mo. Run a free community, build your audience, and upgrade to Pro when you're ready to turn on payments.

Compare that to the "free" Facebook Group where you're paying $150/mo for the tool stack, spending 5 extra hours a week on workarounds, and losing sales because your checkout process has three extra steps.

Free isn't free. It's just a different way of paying.

The real question isn't about cost

The question isn't "why would I pay for Skool?" The question is: "what's my time worth, and how much revenue am I leaving on the table with a patchwork setup?"

If the answer is "not much, I'm just hanging out with friends," then Facebook is great. Keep using it.

But if you're building a business, teaching a skill, or running a paid program, the right tools pay for themselves in the first week. Skool isn't an expense. It's the thing that makes the money possible.

Three members and it's paid for. Everything after that is profit. Not a bad deal for something that replaces five other tools and gives you back hours of your week. 🔥
