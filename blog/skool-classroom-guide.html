How to Build Your Skool Classroom: Courses, Modules & Drip Content

By Izzy Piyale-Sheard·April 14, 2026

You've got a Skool community. People are posting, chatting, maybe even climbing the leaderboard. But where does your actual course content live?

That's what the Classroom is for. It's Skool's built-in course hosting. No need for Teachable, Kajabi, or any other platform bolted on the side. Your courses live right inside your community, and members can access them without leaving the app.

Let's walk through how to build it out, step by step.

What the Classroom Actually Is

The Classroom is one of Skool's two main sections. You've got Community (the discussion feed) and Classroom (your courses). Members switch between them using tabs at the top.

Think of it as a mini course platform baked into your community. You can host multiple courses, organize them into modules and lessons, and track member progress. All without any third-party tools.

This matters because it keeps everything in one place. Members don't need separate logins. They don't need to remember a different URL. They go to your Skool group, click Classroom, and start learning. Zero friction.

The Structure: Courses, Modules, and Lessons

Skool's classroom has a clean three-tier hierarchy:

  • Courses are at the top. Each course is a separate container. You might have one course called "Getting Started" and another called "Advanced Strategies."
  • Modules live inside courses. These are groups of related lessons. Think of them like chapters in a book.
  • Lessons live inside modules. Each lesson is a single page where you put your actual content.

Here's what that looks like in practice:

📚 Course: "Launch Your First Product"

📁 Module 1: Foundations

📄 Lesson 1: Finding Your Niche

📄 Lesson 2: Defining Your Offer

📄 Lesson 3: Pricing Strategy

📁 Module 2: Building

📄 Lesson 1: Creating Your MVP

📄 Lesson 2: Setting Up Payments

📁 Module 3: Launch Week

📄 Lesson 1: Pre-Launch Checklist

📄 Lesson 2: Launch Day Playbook

Simple. Clean. Members can see exactly where they are and what's coming next.

What You Can Put in a Lesson

Each lesson page supports multiple content types. You're not limited to just video or just text.

  • Video. Embed from YouTube, Vimeo, Loom, or upload directly. Video is usually the main attraction.
  • Text. Rich text with formatting, headers, bold, links. Great for written walkthroughs, checklists, or summaries below your video.
  • Files. Attach PDFs, spreadsheets, templates, or any downloadable resource. Members grab them right from the lesson page.
  • Action items. These are checkboxes members can tick off. "Download the template." "Post your niche idea in the community." "Schedule your first call." Action items drive completion and give members a clear next step.

The best lessons combine two or three of these. A video walkthrough, a summary underneath, a downloadable template, and an action item to go apply what they learned. That's a complete lesson.

Drip Content: Schedule Module Releases Over Time

You don't have to dump everything on members at once. Skool lets you drip content, releasing modules on a schedule after someone joins.

Here's how it works. When you create a module, you can set a "drip delay" in days. If you set Module 2 to drip after 7 days, a new member won't see it until one week after they joined your community.

Why drip? A few reasons:

  • Prevents overwhelm. A 30-lesson course dumped all at once is intimidating. Spacing it out keeps members focused on what's in front of them.
  • Builds anticipation. "Module 3 unlocks in 3 days" gives members something to look forward to.
  • Matches your coaching schedule. If you run a group coaching program, you can sync module releases with your weekly calls.
  • Reduces refund requests. Members stay engaged longer when they have new content arriving each week.

A common drip schedule: release one module per week over 4 to 8 weeks. This mirrors a live cohort experience, even if your course is pre-recorded.

The "Start Here" Module: Your Most Important Course

Every Skool community should have a "Start Here" course. This is the first thing new members see when they click into the Classroom tab.

You can set any course as your default course. This is the one that loads first. Make it your onboarding course.

What goes in a good Start Here course:

  • A welcome video (2 to 3 minutes, max). Who you are, what this community is about, what they should do first.
  • Community guidelines. How to get the most out of the group. Where to post questions. What's expected.
  • A quick win. One lesson that delivers immediate value. A template, a checklist, a short tutorial. Something they can act on today.
  • An action item that sends them to the Community tab. "Introduce yourself in the #Welcome channel." This bridges the classroom and community sections.

A strong Start Here course reduces confusion, sets expectations, and gets new members active within their first session. Don't skip this.

Organizing Multiple Courses

Should you create one big course or several smaller ones? Depends on what you're teaching.

Use separate courses when:

  • Topics are distinct. A "Marketing 101" course and a "Sales Scripts" course are different enough to stand alone.
  • You want to lock some courses behind levels. (More on this in a moment.)
  • You plan to add new courses over time as ongoing value for members.

Use modules within one course when:

  • The content follows a linear sequence. Week 1, Week 2, Week 3.
  • You want members to complete things in order.
  • The topics are closely related and build on each other.

Many community owners do both. A main flagship course with sequential modules, plus a few standalone courses for bonus topics, templates, or masterclasses. That's a solid setup.

Locking Courses Behind Levels

This is where the Classroom and Skool's gamification system connect. You can set any course to require a specific level before a member can access it.

When you edit a course, you'll see an option to set a minimum level. Set it to Level 3, and only members who have earned enough points to reach Level 3 can see the content.

Why this works:

  • It rewards active members with bonus content
  • It motivates newer members to participate more
  • It creates a sense of progression. "I unlocked something new" feels great.

A natural setup: your Start Here course and main course are available to everyone. Bonus courses, advanced material, and template libraries are locked behind Level 2 or Level 3. Your best stuff (live call recordings, behind-the-scenes content) goes behind Level 4 or 5.

Members earn access by being active in the community. Not by paying more. That's a different kind of value exchange, and it builds loyalty.

Best Practices for Building Your Classroom

After setting up courses in Skool for a while, here's what works:

Keep modules short. 5 to 10 lessons per module is the sweet spot. More than that and members lose their place. They see a 20-lesson module and think "I'll get to it later." Later usually means never.

Use action items on every lesson. Even if it's just "Watch the video and check this box." Action items give members something to do, and the checkbox completion creates a small dopamine hit. For best results, make at least one action item per lesson require doing something outside the lesson. "Post your draft headline in the community for feedback."

Include a community discussion prompt. At the end of each lesson, add a prompt that sends members to the community feed. "What's your biggest takeaway from this lesson? Share it in the #Wins channel." This creates a bridge between passive learning and active participation. Your course content feeds community engagement, and community engagement feeds course completion. Flywheel.

Put your best content first. Module 1, Lesson 1 should deliver real value. Not a "welcome to the course, let me tell you what we'll cover" fluff lesson. Teach something. Give them a win. If the first thing they experience is good, they'll trust the rest is good too.

Name your modules clearly. "Module 3" tells nobody anything. "Getting Your First Client" tells them exactly what they'll learn. Clear names reduce friction and set expectations.

Don't aim for perfection. A recorded Loom video with good information beats a professionally produced video that takes you three months to publish. Ship it. Improve it later. Your members care about the content, not the production value.

Wrapping Up

Skool's Classroom gives you everything you need to host courses right where your community already lives. No extra tools. No separate logins. No complicated integrations.

The structure is simple: courses hold modules, modules hold lessons. Add video, text, files, and action items. Drip content over time. Lock premium courses behind levels. And always, always include a prompt that sends members back into the community.

Start with a strong "Start Here" course and one main course. Build from there. You can always add more as your community grows. 🚀
