Should You Start a Free or Paid Skool Community?

By Izzy Piyale-Sheard · April 14, 2026

This is the question that freezes people in place for weeks. Free or paid? What if I charge and nobody joins? What if I give it away and nobody values it?

Both are real concerns. And the answer depends on where you are right now, not where you want to be in six months.

Let's break it down so you can pick a path and actually launch.

When free makes sense

Starting free is the right move when you're building from zero. No audience. No email list. No track record of people paying you for your knowledge. If that's you, a free community removes friction and lets you focus on the thing that matters most: getting people in the room.

Free also works when you're testing an idea. Maybe you think there's a market for a community around freelance copywriting, or fitness for people over 50, or DIY home renovation. A free group lets you validate that before you build a whole business around it.

Here's what a free Skool community gives you:

  • Social proof fast. Getting to 100 members in a free group can happen in a few weeks. Getting to 100 paying members takes months.
  • Content that compounds. Every post, every discussion, every resource you share becomes a reason for the next person to join.
  • A testing ground. You learn what your people actually want. Not what you think they want.
  • Skool Discovery visibility. Free communities can rank in Skool's built-in search, bringing in members you didn't have to go find.

The risk? You attract people who want free stuff. Some of them will never pay for anything. That's the tradeoff, and it's real.

When paid makes sense

If you already have an audience (even a small one), charging from day one sends a signal: this is worth something.

Paid communities start smaller but run hotter. The people who pay $29 or $49 or $97 per month show up differently. They post. They engage. They do the work. They stick around.

Go paid from the start if:

  • You have an existing audience. An email list of 500+. A YouTube channel. A podcast. A LinkedIn following. You have people who already trust you.
  • You have clear expertise. You've done the thing. You can teach the thing. People already ask you questions about the thing.
  • You want fewer, better members. A paid group of 30 engaged people beats a free group of 300 lurkers every single day.
  • You're ready to deliver. You have content, a curriculum, or a coaching offer ready to go. Not "I'll figure it out later." Now.

The numbers back this up. Paid Skool communities consistently see 60-80% of members actively engaging. Free communities? Closer to 10-20%. That gap changes everything about how it feels to run your group.

The hybrid model (this is what most smart creators do)

Here's the move that gets overlooked: run both.

Free community on the front end. Paid tier on the back end.

Your free group is the top of your funnel. People discover you, get value, see the culture, and build trust. Then you offer something premium: a paid challenge, a VIP tier, a coaching upgrade.

This works because of how Skool is built. You can run multiple groups on one account. So your free group feeds your paid group naturally. Members level up when they're ready.

What this looks like in practice:

  1. Free community with valuable discussions, a basic course in the classroom, and weekly posts from you
  2. Paid group ($47-$97/month) with live coaching calls, advanced training, direct access to you, and accountability
  3. Paid challenges ($47-$197 one-time) that run inside either group as time-limited sprints

The free group builds the audience. The paid group builds the business. The challenges create urgency and cash injections. Three layers working together.

The "freeloader" problem (and why it's overblown)

People worry about this a lot. "If I make it free, I'll just attract freeloaders."

Here's the reality: in any community, free or paid, about 10% of members will be highly active. Another 20-30% will engage occasionally. The rest will lurk. This is normal. It's how communities work everywhere, from Reddit to private masterminds.

The difference is what those lurkers are worth to you.

In a free group, lurkers are still eyeballs. They see your posts. They see your offers. Some of them will buy when the time is right. One quiet member who buys your $997 course is worth more than fifty active members who never spend a dime.

In a paid group, lurkers are still paying. A member who pays $49/month but never posts is still $588/year in revenue. If they're getting value by reading, that's fine.

The real freeloader problem isn't freeloaders. It's running a free group with no path to revenue. That burns you out. Don't do that. Always have something to sell.

Conversion rates: what to expect

If you run a free group and want to convert members to a paid offer, here are realistic numbers:

  • Free to paid membership: 2-5% of your free members will convert to a monthly subscription. So 200 free members might produce 4-10 paying members.
  • Free to paid challenge: 5-15% conversion is common for a well-run challenge launch. Urgency and deadlines help.
  • Free to high-ticket offer: 1-3% is typical. But at $997+ per sale, you don't need many.

These numbers go up dramatically when you're active in the community, when your free content is genuinely useful, and when your paid offer has a clear transformation attached to it.

So which should you pick?

Here's the quick decision tree:

No audience yet? Start free. Get 50-100 members. Learn what they want. Build your first paid offer based on what you learn. Then launch a paid tier.

Small audience (500+ email subscribers or social followers)? Go paid from day one at a low price ($19-$49/month). Your existing audience gives you enough runway to start with quality over quantity.

Established audience (2,000+ followers)? Go hybrid. Free group for reach, paid group for revenue. Run a paid challenge in your first month to create momentum.

Already selling courses or coaching? Go paid. Period. Your community is an extension of what you already do. Don't dilute it by making it free.

The worst choice is no choice. Spending three months deliberating about free vs. paid while someone else in your niche launches and grabs your future members.

Pick one. Launch. Adjust later. Skool makes it easy to switch models, add tiers, or pivot entirely. The platform won't punish you for changing your mind. But the market will punish you for not starting. 🚀
