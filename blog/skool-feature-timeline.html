Every Feature Skool Has Added: A Complete Timeline (2019-2026)

By Izzy Piyale-Sheard · April 14, 2026

Skool has gone from a closed beta with a handful of users to one of the most talked-about community platforms on the internet. But the growth didn't happen overnight. It was a slow, deliberate build over seven years.

This post tracks every major feature and milestone from Skool's founding through today. Think of it as the full origin story, told through the product decisions that shaped the platform.

A note on dates: Skool doesn't publish a traditional public changelog. Some dates below are exact (sourced from official announcements and community posts). Others are approximate, based on community reports and third-party reviews. Where I couldn't verify a specific date, I've grouped features by period (e.g., "Early 2024") rather than guess.

2019: The idea takes shape

July 2019: Sam Ovens and Daniel Kang co-found Skool

Sam Ovens had been running Consulting.com, a large online education business. He was frustrated with juggling separate tools for his community, courses, and live calls. His pitch was simple: what if all of that lived in one place?

He teamed up with Daniel Kang (CTO) and started building. Ovens reportedly spent over $700,000 per month on development during this phase and interviewed more than 600 people for the CTO role before landing on Kang. This wasn't a side project.

Late 2019: Closed beta launch

Skool opened to a small, invite-only group of beta users. The initial version was bare bones: a community feed, a basic classroom for courses, and a calendar. But the core architecture was already there. Four tabs. One clean interface. No bloat.

2020-2021: The quiet years

Extended beta period

Skool stayed in closed beta for over two years. During this time, the team refined the four core pillars that still define the platform today:

  • Community: A chronological feed with post categories, comments, and member profiles.
  • Classroom: Course hosting with modules, lessons, video embeds (YouTube, Vimeo, Loom), and file attachments.
  • Calendar: Event scheduling with automatic time zone conversion and Zoom integration.
  • Gamification: Points, levels, and leaderboards. Members earn points for posting, commenting, and completing courses. Levels unlock perks defined by the community owner.

The gamification system was built into Skool from the start, not bolted on later. This is one of the things that separates it from platforms that added engagement features as an afterthought.

These were quiet years externally, but the product was maturing fast behind closed doors. The small beta group gave the team tight feedback loops to iterate quickly.

2022: Going public

Early 2022: Public launch

After roughly two and a half years of closed beta, Skool opened its doors to anyone. The platform launched with a single pricing plan (no tiers, no free option). The message was clear: this is a serious tool for serious community builders.

The public launch included all the core features refined during beta:

  • Community feed with categories and pinned posts
  • Course builder with unlimited modules and lessons
  • Event calendar with Zoom integration
  • Points, levels, and leaderboard gamification
  • Payment processing via Stripe Express for paid communities
  • Built-in discovery page where people could browse and find communities

Growth was slow at first. Skool didn't have the brand recognition of Teachable or Kajabi. But the simplicity of the product started attracting creators tired of managing a tangled web of separate tools.

Drip content and course permissions

Skool added the ability to drip-release course modules over time. Instead of giving members access to everything at once, creators could set content to unlock X days after a member joins. This was a big deal for structured programs, cohort-based courses, and retention. It turned the classroom from a content library into a paced learning experience.

2023: Growth accelerates

Skool's popularity takes off

2023 was the year Skool went from "that platform some people use" to "the platform everyone's talking about." The creator economy was booming. Community-driven courses were replacing traditional online courses. And Skool's simplicity hit the right nerve at the right time.

Mid 2023: Mobile apps (iOS and Android)

Skool launched native mobile apps for both iOS and Android. Before this, members had to use the mobile web browser, which worked but wasn't great for engagement. The native apps gave members push notifications, a faster interface, and the habit-forming "open the app and check in" behavior that drives daily engagement.

The iOS app launched on the App Store under "Skool Communities." Android followed shortly after on Google Play.

Affiliate program (40% recurring commissions)

Skool rolled out its affiliate program, offering 40% recurring commissions for referring new community creators to the platform. That's not a one-time fee. It's 40% of the subscription price, every month, for as long as the referred creator stays on Skool. On the Pro plan, that's about $39/mo per referral.

The program comes with a 14-day cookie window and payouts via FirstPromoter once you hit a $100 threshold. This turned Skool's existing users into a growth engine. Creators weren't just using Skool. They were actively telling other creators to use it too.

Improved notification controls and admin settings

Quality-of-life updates arrived throughout the year: better control over which notifications members receive, the ability to pin multiple posts (not just one), and improved admin permissions. Small individually, but together they smoothed out the day-to-day experience of running a community.

2024: The Hormozi effect

January 2024: Alex Hormozi invests in Skool

On January 10, 2024, Alex Hormozi announced what he called "the largest investment of my life" into Skool.com. Hormozi is the founder of Acquisition.com and a massive online brand in the business/fitness space. His involvement brought two things: serious credibility and a huge influx of new users.

Hormozi didn't just invest money. He became an active presence on the platform, using Skool for his own communities and bringing his audience along. The partnership between Ovens (product builder) and Hormozi (audience builder) turned out to be a powerful combo.

February 2024: Skool Games launches

The Skool Games debuted on February 2, 2024, with a live opening ceremony. It's a competition among community owners: grow your Skool group as fast as possible over a set period. Winners get prizes, recognition, and bragging rights. The competition runs monthly and quarterly.

This was smart for two reasons. First, it gamified the platform at the operator level, not just the member level. Community owners were now competing with each other. Second, it generated massive organic content. Participants shared their strategies, results, and stories across YouTube, Twitter, and podcasts. Free marketing at scale.

Mid 2024: 7-day free trial for members

Skool added the ability for community owners to offer a 7-day free trial to new members. Toggle it on in your pricing settings, and potential members can access your community for a week before their card gets charged. The feature also included trial tracking and conversion rate analytics so you could see how many trial members converted to paid.

This was a direct response to creator feedback. A lot of people wanted to let prospects "try before they buy" without the hassle of manual coupon codes or workarounds.

Mid-Late 2024: Member location data and request screening

When someone requests to join your community, you can now see their location. Useful for understanding your audience's geography, deciding whether someone's a good fit, and spotting suspicious join requests.

Late 2024: Enhanced analytics

Skool expanded its analytics dashboard with lifetime value tracking for members, improved revenue dashboards, and one-time sales tracking. Community owners could now see metrics that matter for running a business, not just vanity engagement numbers.

2025: Feature explosion

2025 was the year Skool went from "community platform" to "community business platform." Feature after feature dropped, each one aimed at giving creators more control over how they monetize and run their groups.

Early 2025: Hobby plan ($9/mo)

For years, Skool had a single price point. You paid $99/mo or you didn't use Skool. That changed with the introduction of the Hobby plan at $9/mo.

The Hobby plan includes unlimited members, unlimited courses, unlimited videos, live calls, and a custom URL. The catch: a 10% transaction fee on payments and no access to premium features like webinars. It also comes with a 14-day free trial.

This was a game-changer for the entry point. People who wanted to test Skool without the $99 commitment could now start building for less than a Netflix subscription. Upgrade to Pro when the revenue justifies it.

Early 2025: One-time course payments

Skool used to be subscription-only. Every community charged a monthly fee or was free. Now creators can sell individual courses for a one-time price. This opened the door for a whole new pricing model: free community with paid courses inside it.

The settings now offer five monetization models: Free, Subscription, Freemium (free entry, paid upgrades), Tiers, and One-time payment. Much more flexible than the original "monthly subscription or nothing" approach.

February 2025: Discovery page overhaul

Skool revamped its community discovery page with nine new categories and a redesigned browsing experience. The top 5 groups in each category now rotate in random order, giving smaller communities a better chance at visibility instead of the same big groups dominating the top spots forever.

This is Skool leaning into its marketplace potential. They're not just a tool. They're becoming a place where people discover communities organically.

Mid 2025: Moderator roles

Community owners can now assign moderator roles. Moderators can approve member join requests and organize posts, but they can't ban members or change roles. It's a limited permission set, but it lets owners delegate day-to-day management without giving away the keys.

Mid 2025: Post deletion with explanations

Admins can now delete posts and include a reason. The member gets an email explaining what happened and why. A small feature, but it solves a real moderation pain point. Instead of "your post disappeared and you have no idea why," members get transparency.

Mid 2025: Enhanced post reporting

When members report a post, they can now include a reason. Admins get context instead of a bare "this was reported" flag. Makes moderation decisions faster and fairer.

October 2025: Membership tiers (Standard, Premium, VIP)

This was one of the most requested features in Skool's history. Community owners can now create up to three membership tiers with different pricing and benefits. The tier names are standardized (Standard, Premium, VIP) to keep things consistent across the platform.

Higher tiers automatically include all benefits from lower tiers. So a VIP member gets everything in Premium and Standard too. The tiers show up at signup, letting new members choose their level. Drag-and-drop benefit management makes it easy to set up, and a "view as member" mode lets you preview what each tier sees.

This unlocked pricing strategies that weren't possible before. A free community tier for discovery, a $49/mo Standard tier for courses, and a $199/mo VIP tier for group coaching and direct access. All in one group.

October 27, 2025: Native webinars and live streaming

Skool launched native one-to-many live streaming for up to 10,000 participants. This is separate from the existing group calls feature. The host controls the stage, can bring attendees up to speak, and the audience can react and chat in real-time.

This was the feature that eliminated the last common reason to use Zoom alongside Skool. Launches, summits, podcast recordings, large Q&A sessions. All inside the platform. No more "click this Zoom link" workarounds. Webinars are available on the Pro plan. The Hobby plan keeps the smaller meetings feature.

Late 2025: Google Ads tracking

Community owners running paid ads can now track Google Ads performance directly inside Skool. See how your ad spend is converting into community sign-ups in real time. For anyone spending money to grow their group, this is essential data that previously required third-party tracking tools.

Late 2025: Dark mode

Skool rolled out dark mode across the platform, including a dedicated dark mode icon for the iOS app. Functional rather than flashy, but it's one of those features that a surprising number of users had been asking for.

2026 (so far): Platform maturation

Discovery as ecosystem

Skool has been doubling down on its discovery features. The platform is positioning itself not just as a tool you build on, but as a marketplace where people browse communities by interest. Think of it like an app store for learning communities. People can search, preview, join free groups, and upgrade to paid ones without ever leaving Skool.

Anti-spam and identity verification

With growth comes spam. Skool has invested heavily in anti-spam measures and "authentic identity" policies. The result: communities on Skool feel notably cleaner than equivalent Facebook Groups. Fewer bots. Fewer promotional drive-bys. More real people actually engaging.

Coming soon (announced but not yet live)

  • Apple Pay and Google Pay: More payment options for members at checkout.
  • Badges: Visual recognition for active members, separate from the level system.
  • Credit card requirement for free trials: To reduce fake sign-ups and ensure trial members are real prospects.

The big picture

Looking at this timeline, a few patterns stand out:

Patience over speed. Skool spent two and a half years in beta before going public. Most startups would have launched in six months. That extended development period meant the core product was solid from day one.

Simplicity is a feature. Other platforms kept adding tabs, menus, and options. Skool kept it to four tabs and slowly expanded what those tabs could do. The interface today looks almost identical to the 2022 launch. That consistency matters.

Monetization got flexible. Skool started with one model (monthly subscription). Now it supports five (free, subscription, freemium, tiers, one-time payments). Each addition opened the door for a new type of creator.

The Hormozi inflection point. January 2024 was clearly the moment Skool went mainstream. The investment brought attention, users, and validation. But the product was ready for that spotlight because of the years of quiet building that came before it.

Seven years in, Skool is still adding features at a fast clip. But the core philosophy hasn't changed: community, courses, calendar, gamification. One platform. No bloat. Everything you need, nothing you don't.
